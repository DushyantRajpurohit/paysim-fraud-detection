1The too-good score

PaySim is a simulator of mobile-money transfers: 6,362,620 transactions, 8,213 of them fraud. I trained LightGBM on everything a payment system knows before money moves (the amount, the balances, the hour, the destination account's history) and scored it on the last 15% of hours, which it had never seen.

I report PR-AUC (the area under the precision-recall curve) because fraud here is rare, about 3 in every 1,000 transfers. Accuracy would read 99.7% for a model that flags nothing at all; PR-AUC only rewards finding the fraud.

My first model scored 0.980 PR-AUC. Then I opened the saved model to see what it had learned. Early stopping had kept 1 tree.

A model that good with that little inside it isn't detecting fraud. It's applying a rule. So I went looking for the rule.

2What’s in the data

Two things stood out before any modelling. First, fraud only ever happens in two of the five transaction types, transfers and cash-outs. The other three never contain a single fraud, so the model doesn't score them at all.

Second, the simulation isn't stable over time. Fraud arrives at a steady rate, but ordinary traffic collapses a little past halfway.

That's why I split by time instead of at random. Training covers hours 1–323, validation 324–377, and the test set 378–743. The fraud rate goes from 0.19% in training to 0.98% in test. A random split would have hidden that shift; a real payment system lives with it.

3The trick

PaySim's fraudster always takes everything. In 97.8% of frauds, the amount equals the origin account's balance to the last unit. Among 2.76 million legitimate transfers and cash-outs, that happens not once.

amount == oldbalanceOrg

On its own, that comparison scores 0.975 PR-AUC. Every feature that shows the model the origin balance lets it rediscover the same comparison, which is exactly what my one-tree model did. So a 0.99 on PaySim tells you very little: one comparison gets you almost all the way there.

Real fraud doesn't empty accounts this tidily. A model that learns this rule has learned PaySim.

4Taking it away

So the model I serve never sees the origin balance: no oldbalanceOrg, no amount-to-balance ratio, no "empties the account" flag. What's left is the amount, the hour, the transfer type, and the destination's side: its balance and how much it has received before.

The honest number is 0.634 PR-AUC. At my default threshold it catches 77.0% of frauds and 96.4% of the money involved, because large frauds score higher than small ones.

Set aside: the balances after the payment

Adding the post-transaction balances pushes the score to 0.999. But a payment system has to decide before money moves, when those balances don't exist yet. I trained that model only to show the size of the leak.

5Doing it properly

Once the shortcut was gone, every choice had to be earned. I made each one on three rolling time windows: train on everything before a cut-off, score the next window, all of them ending before the test period begins. The test set only gets scored once, at the end.

Two changes held up: adding the destination's history (how much it has received before, and how this payment compares with its usual), and requiring at least 1,000 transactions in every leaf of every tree. Bigger, looser trees chase patterns that only exist in particular hours.

Set aside: "hours since the account last got paid"

It scored better on the windows (0.458). It also drifts badly: as the simulation runs, accounts go longer between payments, so the test period shows values training never saw (drift score 0.967, where anything above 0.25 counts as drifted). I only noticed because my test score dropped when I added it, and looking at the test set is exactly what you're not supposed to do. So I turned the lesson into a rule that needs no labels: drift on recent traffic can always be measured, and any feature past 0.25 is out, whatever its validation score.

6Where to draw the line

A model gives a score; a business decides what to do with it. A missed fraud costs its full amount. A false alarm costs an analyst's time. I pick the threshold that makes the total cost smallest on the validation days, then report what it does on the test days.

Drag the cost of one review and watch the trade-off move.

At a review cost of 10,000, I'd send 10,487 transactions to review. 29.4% of them are fraud, and they hold 96.4% of the stolen money.

8What this isn’t

Limits, plainly

  • PaySim is synthetic. The lesson is about the dataset. 0.634 is the honest number for PaySim, not a claim about real payments.
  • Destination history is passed in by the caller. In production it would come from a feature store.
  • The review cost is an assumption. That's why the slider exists instead of one number.
